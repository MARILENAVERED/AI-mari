# EGFR Mutation Subtypes and Clinical Outcomes in NSCLC (2022–2026): An Evidence Synthesis

## Research question and scope

**Question:** What are the most recent peer-reviewed studies (2022–2026)
evaluating EGFR mutation subtypes and their association with treatment
response, acquired resistance, and clinical outcomes in patients with
non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)?

**Population:** Adult NSCLC patients with molecularly confirmed EGFR
mutations — common subtypes (exon 19 deletion, L858R) and uncommon
subtypes (exon 20 insertion, G719X, L861Q, S768I, and compound mutations).

**Intervention/exposure:** EGFR mutation subtype as a variable, generally
in the context of EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy.

**Comparator:** Other EGFR subtypes, wild-type EGFR, or other treatment
arms, where reported.

**Outcomes:** Treatment response (ORR, PFS, OS), acquired resistance
mechanisms, and subtype-associated clinical outcomes.

**Study types included:** Clinical cohort studies, clinical trials, and
retrospective/prospective analyses reporting subtype-stratified outcomes.
Per an updated study-type criterion in `research-question.md`,
**narrative reviews are excluded as evidence** in this report — three
records identified during literature search (Ferro et al. 2024, Spitaleri
et al. 2026, Zullo & Remon 2026) were reclassified as excluded at intake
on this basis and do not appear as cited support anywhere below.

**Exclusion criteria applied:** non-NSCLC cancers, animal-only/in-vitro
studies without clinical correlate; single-patient case reports (where
other evidence existed); studies reporting only pooled "EGFR-mutant" data
without subtype stratification; preprints/conference-abstract-only
sources; and narrative reviews without a stated search methodology.

**Date range:** 2022–2026. **Language:** English.

This report is based on **8 primary-data studies** that passed both
literature-scout's bibliographic verification (Gate 1) and
evidence-reviewer's inclusion screening (Gate 2). No narrative review is
cited as evidentiary support anywhere in this report.

## Methods summary

Literature was identified by `literature-scout` through five PubMed
searches (`mcp__PubMed__search_articles`), spanning: (1) EGFR
subtype/treatment-response generally, (2) EGFR exon 20 insertion
outcomes, (3) uncommon point-mutation subtypes (G719X/L861Q/S768I), (4)
acquired resistance mechanisms by subtype, and (5) compound EGFR
mutations — all restricted to a 2022–2026 publication-date window. Across
these runs, 24 unique PMIDs were verified against PubMed's authoritative
metadata (title, authors, journal, year, DOI/PMCID). Records were excluded
at the search/intake stage for reasons including: case reports where other
evidence existed, pooled (non-subtype-stratified) EGFR-mutant cohorts,
preclinical-only studies, off-scope comparisons, and out-of-date-range
publications (full reasons recorded per-record in `sources/records/` and
in `sources/search-log.md`).

Following a post-hoc scope correction (research-question.md updated to
exclude narrative reviews as evidence), three previously-forwarded review
articles were reclassified as excluded at intake. `evidence-reviewer`
then applied the research question's inclusion/exclusion criteria to the
remaining verified/partial records, extracting structured evidence into
`outputs/evidence-table.md`: **8 studies included**, all with primary
clinical/genomic data (7 observational — retrospective or single-arm
prospective — cohort studies, and 1 randomized controlled trial), 3
narrative reviews excluded as evidence.

A bookkeeping gap was found and corrected during report preparation: one
of the 9 records still marked "Pass forward" after the post-hoc review
correction (Jing et al., PMID 42453333) had, in fact, already been
excluded by `evidence-reviewer` in an earlier screening pass (its primary
exposure is concurrent driver-gene co-mutation status, not EGFR-subtype-
stratified outcomes) — its source-record disposition line was simply
never updated to reflect that at the time. This has been corrected in
`sources/records/PMID-42453333.md` and `sources/search-log.md`. Net
result: 9 forwarded minus 1 (Jing, excluded) minus the count already
reflected in the evidence table's 8 included + 3 excluded-review rows
reconciles cleanly — 8 studies proceed to evidence extraction.

## Evidence synthesis

### 1. First-line efficacy in EGFR exon 20 insertion NSCLC (randomized trial evidence)

The strongest-design evidence in this set is a phase 3, international,
randomized controlled trial (WU-KONG28) comparing first-line
sunvozertinib against carboplatin-pemetrexed chemotherapy in 324 patients
with EGFR exon 20 insertion-positive NSCLC (Zhou et al., 2026). Sunvozertinib
significantly improved blinded independent central review-assessed PFS
(median 10.3 vs. 7.5 months; HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.50–0.85, P<0.001), 12-month
PFS (46.1% vs. 26.7%), ORR (58.9% vs. 31.1%), and duration of response
(11.2 vs. 7.1 months) relative to chemotherapy. OS data were immature
(38.9% maturity) at the time of reporting, so a survival benefit is not
yet established. Grade ≥3 adverse events were more frequent with
sunvozertinib (75.5% vs. 56.7%), representing a tolerability trade-off
(Zhou et al., 2026).

This is the only randomized trial in the evidence base; it is
industry-funded (Dizal Pharmaceuticals) and reports a first-line,
treatment-naive population specifically with exon 20 insertion mutations.

### 2. Later-line, real-world efficacy in EGFR exon 20 insertion NSCLC

Two studies examined later-line, non-randomized treatment of exon 20
insertion NSCLC with newer targeted agents:

- **Amivantamab**, used in subsequent lines after chemotherapy ± immunotherapy,
  was evaluated in the Italian ATLAS real-world registry (Passiglia et al.,
  2026; n=64/119 registry patients treated). Reported outcomes: ORR 37.5%,
  disease control rate 66.2%, median PFS 9.6 months, median OS 16.9 months.
  In a brain-metastasis subgroup (n=23), 13% had a partial intracranial
  response and intracranial median PFS was 11.6 months. Treatment-related
  adverse events occurred in 68.8% (any grade) and 10.9% (grade 3–4),
  mainly skin rash (Passiglia et al., 2026).
- **Mobocertinib** was evaluated in a single-center, prospective,
  paired-ctDNA study of 22 patients (Kim et al., 2026), reporting ORR 59%,
  disease control rate 82%, and median PFS 5.6 months (95% CI 3.5–9.3).
  Among 14 patients with prior amivantamab exposure, ORR was 57.1% with
  median PFS 5.8 months. Responses were better in helical-region insertion
  sites, and baseline ctDNA-negative status correlated with more favorable
  outcomes.

**Interpretation:** These two studies describe efficacy in heavily
pretreated, later-line, real-world or single-center populations using
different agents (amivantamab, mobocertinib) than the first-line RCT
population above (sunvozertinib). Per the evidence table's explicit
caution, these figures should **not be compared head-to-head** with the
WU-KONG28 first-line RCT results, nor with each other as agent-vs-agent
evidence, because they differ in line of therapy, study design (no
concurrent comparator arm in either), population selection, and (for
Kim et al.) sample size (Passiglia et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026; Zhou
et al., 2026).

### 3. Acquired resistance mechanisms in EGFR exon 20 insertion NSCLC

Kim et al. (2026) used paired pre-/post-treatment ctDNA sequencing in
mobocertinib-treated patients (n=22) and found diverse resistance
mechanisms at progression: EGFR amplification, RTK/RAS pathway
alterations, and rarer events including gene fusion and small-cell
transformation. This is a single-center, small-sample, observational
finding without a comparator arm (Kim et al., 2026).

### 4. Natural history and prognostic co-factors in EGFR exon 20 insertion NSCLC

Reyes et al. (2026) characterized CNS disease patterns and co-mutation
status in a retrospective cohort of 80 exon 20 insertion NSCLC patients.
CNS progression occurred in 36.25% and leptomeningeal disease in 7.5%.
Neither CNS progression status (median OS 45.3 vs. 47.8 months; HR 1.21,
95% CI 0.66–2.23, P=0.54) nor TP53 co-mutation status (median OS 31.6 vs.
47.8 months; HR 1.79, 95% CI 0.89–3.60, P=0.11) was associated with a
**statistically significant** difference in overall survival. Both
comparisons had wide confidence intervals crossing 1. **These findings
must be read as non-significant, hypothesis-generating observations, not
as established prognostic effects** — the numerically lower survival
with TP53 co-mutation and with CNS progression should not be presented as
confirmed associations (Reyes et al., 2026).

### 5. Uncommon point mutations (G719X, L861Q, S768I) and compound mutations: TKI choice

Three independent studies, using different comparators, agents, and
endpoints, converge on a signal that **compound EGFR mutations and
specific uncommon subtypes may respond differently depending on TKI
choice** — but none provides a single, poolable quantitative estimate, and
this should be treated as a convergent-but-distinct signal rather than a
unified effect size:

- **Shigematsu et al. (2025)**, a retrospective multicenter cohort of 35
  patients with uncommon EGFR mutations (G719X, L861Q, S768I, compound),
  found that later-generation (2nd/3rd-gen) TKI use was associated with
  significantly longer OS than non-use (47.7 vs. 15.5 months; p=0.0177),
  with liver metastases and poor performance status as independent poor
  prognostic factors. Descriptively, afatinib showed favorable treatment
  duration specifically in the G719X and compound-mutation subgroups
  (Shigematsu et al., 2025).
- **Nagano et al. (2026)**, a 29-hospital Japanese retrospective cohort
  (n=162; afatinib n=95 vs. osimertinib n=67) using inverse probability of
  treatment weighting, found **no significant overall difference** between
  afatinib and osimertinib in weighted time to treatment failure (HR 1.04,
  95% CI 0.61–1.77) or OS (HR 1.34, 95% CI 0.73–2.44). Afatinib was
  associated with higher response rates but more dose-reduction-requiring
  adverse events. A subgroup analysis suggested osimertinib favored L861X
  mutations while afatinib favored compound mutations — **but the abstract
  does not report subgroup sample sizes, hazard ratios, or p-values for
  this subgroup finding, so it should be treated as descriptive and
  hypothesis-generating, not as a confirmed subtype-specific treatment
  effect** (Nagano et al., 2026).
- **Lu et al. (2026)**, a brief report combining an institutional cohort
  (UC San Diego Health) with the AACR GENIE database, found that L858R
  patients were more likely than exon19del patients to harbor a de novo
  compound mutation (24/159 [15%] vs. 4/228 [2%], P<.01), and that
  acquired C797S more often co-occurred with exon19del than L858R after
  osimertinib progression (23/113 [20%] vs. 6/89 [7%], P<.01). In the
  GENIE database, 88% of S768I-mutated patients had an additional EGFR
  co-mutation, most commonly G719X. Descriptively, osimertinib time to
  treatment failure was longer for compound G719X/L861Q (median >13
  months) than for G719X alone (median <7 months) — **this TTF comparison
  is presented in the source only as a descriptive median comparison,
  without a formal hazard ratio, confidence interval, or p-value, and
  should not be treated as a statistically confirmed difference** (Lu et
  al., 2026).

**Interpretation:** Taken together, these three studies — using afatinib,
osimertinib, and different comparator structures — all point toward
compound-mutation and specific uncommon-subtype status mattering for TKI
selection and outcome, and specifically toward afatinib performing
relatively favorably in compound-mutation subgroups across two
independent cohorts (Shigematsu et al., 2025; Nagano et al., 2026).
However, because the three studies differ in agents compared, endpoints
reported, and statistical rigor of the subgroup analyses (with two of the
three subgroup findings lacking formal subgroup n/HR/CI/p-values), this
should be read as a **convergent signal warranting further prospective
study, not a single, mergeable quantitative estimate** of a
subtype-by-TKI interaction effect.

### 6. Genomic resistance mechanisms to afatinib by mutation status

Lin et al. (2025) conducted a retrospective genomic cohort study (targeted
NGS on paired baseline/post-progression samples) in 37 patients with
EGFR- or ERBB2-activating mutations on first-line afatinib. PFS was longer
in the EGFR-mutated than the ERBB2-mutated group (HR 0.35, 95% CI
0.15–0.79, p=0.008). Primary resistance was associated with EGFR exon 20
insertion (p=0.011), higher tumor mutational burden (p=0.009), and higher
APOBEC mutational signature (p=0.012) — features enriched in the
ERBB2-mutated cohort. Baseline EGFR exon 19 deletion, together with higher
chromosomal instability and age-related mutational signatures, was
associated with development of known acquired-resistance mechanisms
(T790M, MET amplification) (Lin et al., 2025).

**Flag:** This cohort mixes EGFR-mutated and ERBB2-mutated patients, and
the published abstract does not separately report the EGFR-only subgroup
size or an EGFR-subtype-specific (e.g., exon19del vs. exon20ins) PFS
comparison independent of the EGFR-vs-ERBB2 contrast. This is an
abstract-level data gap in the source, not an extraction choice, and
readers should not treat the EGFR-vs-ERBB2 PFS comparison as equivalent to
a within-EGFR subtype comparison.

## Quality and limitations

**Overall design mix:** Of the 8 included studies, only one is a
randomized controlled trial (Zhou et al., 2026, WU-KONG28) — and it is
industry-funded with immature OS data. The remaining seven studies are
observational: five retrospective cohorts (Shigematsu et al. 2025; Lin et
al. 2025; Nagano et al. 2026; Reyes et al. 2026; Lu et al. 2026), one
retrospective real-world registry (Passiglia et al. 2026), and one small
single-center prospective observational study (Kim et al. 2026). Given
this heterogeneity in design, population, agent, line of therapy, and
statistical rigor, **this report does not present pooled effect
estimates across studies** — each finding is attributed to its specific
study and design context throughout.

**Sample sizes are generally small.** Six of the eight studies have
samples at or below 80 patients (35, 37, 162 [only 162 is >100], 22, 80,
and the compound-mutation-specific institutional subgroups in Lu et al.
ranging 89–228). Subgroup analyses within these already-small cohorts
(e.g., Nagano et al.'s L861X and compound-mutation subgroups; Lu et al.'s
G719X/L861Q vs. G719X-alone TTF comparison) are reported without
subgroup-specific sample sizes, hazard ratios, or p-values in the source
abstracts, and are explicitly flagged in the evidence table as likely
underpowered or not formally tested. This report treats them as
descriptive/hypothesis-generating rather than as confirmed treatment-effect
differences.

**Statistical non-significance is not smoothed over.** Reyes et al.'s
(2026) CNS-progression and TP53 co-mutation survival comparisons both
failed to reach statistical significance (p=0.54 and p=0.11 respectively,
with confidence intervals crossing 1); this report presents both as
non-significant findings, not as established prognostic associations.

**Cross-population comparability is limited.** The exon 20
insertion-specific evidence spans a first-line RCT (Zhou et al.) and two
later-line/real-world studies (Passiglia et al.; Kim et al.) using
different agents; efficacy figures across these should not be compared
head-to-head as if measuring the same clinical scenario. Similarly, Lin
et al.'s mixed EGFR/ERBB2 cohort limits the specificity of its
EGFR-subtype conclusions.

**Convergent but non-mergeable signal on compound mutations.**
Shigematsu et al., Nagano et al., and Lu et al. each report findings
consistent with compound-mutation subtype affecting TKI outcome, but use
different agents, comparators, and endpoints; this report treats their
convergence as a signal supporting the general hypothesis that compound
mutations warrant TKI-specific consideration, without merging their
individual figures into a single quantitative claim.

**Geographic and setting concentration.** Multiple studies are
single-country (Japan: Shigematsu et al., Nagano et al.; Italy: Passiglia
et al.; South Korea: Kim et al.; US: Lin et al., Reyes et al., Lu et al.),
limiting generalizability of subtype-specific findings across
populations and healthcare settings.

## Gaps and uncertainty

- **No head-to-head randomized comparison exists across uncommon EGFR
  subtypes or between afatinib/osimertinib for uncommon mutations.** The
  only RCT in the evidence base (Zhou et al., 2026) is restricted to exon
  20 insertion; the afatinib-vs-osimertinib comparisons for other uncommon
  subtypes (Nagano et al., 2026) are IPTW-adjusted retrospective
  comparisons, not randomized.
- **Subtype-specific subgroup effects for L861X, compound mutations
  (Nagano et al.), and G719X/L861Q vs. G719X-alone (Lu et al.) lack
  reported subgroup sample sizes, hazard ratios, confidence intervals, or
  p-values** in the source abstracts. The evidence base cannot currently
  support a confirmed, quantified claim about these specific
  subtype-by-TKI interactions; this is a gap that would require
  full-text or original-data access, or dedicated prospective study, to
  close.
- **The EGFR-specific subgroup size and subtype-specific PFS breakdown
  are not available** in Lin et al.'s (2025) mixed EGFR/ERBB2 cohort at
  the abstract level — a genuine data gap rather than an extraction
  choice.
- **Long-term survival benefit of sunvozertinib over chemotherapy in
  first-line exon 20 insertion NSCLC is not yet established**; OS data in
  Zhou et al. (2026) were only 38.9% mature at the time of reporting.
- **CNS-progression and TP53 co-mutation prognostic associations in exon
  20 insertion NSCLC (Reyes et al., 2026) remain statistically
  unresolved** — the evidence available neither confirms nor rules out a
  clinically meaningful effect given the wide confidence intervals.
- **No study in this evidence base provides a formal, adequately powered,
  direct comparison of first-line vs. later-line treatment efficacy for
  the same agent or the same population** in exon 20 insertion NSCLC; the
  RCT and real-world studies address different clinical scenarios.
